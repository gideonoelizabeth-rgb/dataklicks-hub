The Skills That Get You Hired in Data and AI

The skills hiring managers look for, how they test them, and a 90-day plan to build proof of work.

By DataKlicks Hub Published 19 November 2026 Last updated 19 November 2026 5 min read

If you want a job in data or AI, you have probably been told to collect certificates, learn five programming languages and follow every new tool. Most hiring managers care about something simpler: can you do the work, and can you show them? This guide sets out the skills that matter most, how employers tend to test them, and a realistic plan to build them.

The skills that matter

  1. Data foundations. Excel for cleaning and summarising data, and SQL for getting data out of databases. These two open more doors than any other pair of skills, and both are often tested in interviews.
  2. Basic statistics and careful thinking. Averages, percentages, trends, and the difference between correlation and cause. These stop you from reporting a number that looks impressive but means little.
  3. A dashboard tool. Power BI or Tableau lets you turn tables into charts and dashboards that answer a question quickly.
  4. Python, when you are ready. Libraries such as pandas help with larger datasets and repeated analysis. Learn it after Excel and SQL, not instead of them.
  5. AI fluency. Using an AI assistant to work faster, and checking what it gives you. In the same way, understanding where simple automation can save time.
  6. Business understanding. Knowing what matters to the business, such as revenue, profit margin, customer retention or late payments, so that your analysis answers a real question.
  7. Communication. Writing a clear summary and explaining it to someone who is not technical. Many capable people lose out here.
  8. Professional habits. Keeping tidy files, writing down what you did, meeting deadlines and handling data responsibly.

Which skills for which role

Job titles and requirements vary between companies, so read each job description carefully. As a general guide, these are the skills that usually carry the most weight.

RoleUsually emphasises
Data analystExcel, SQL, a dashboard tool, clear communication, business understanding
Business intelligence or reporting analystDashboards, data modelling, SQL, reliable and repeatable reports
Business or operations analystExcel, process thinking, simple automation, communication
AI and automation specialist for businessPrompt design, workflow design, automation tools, judgement about risk and data privacy

How employers check your skills

Hiring steps differ, but you will often meet some of these.

  • CV and portfolio review. Do your projects show real analysis rather than a list of tools?
  • A practical test. For example, a short SQL or Excel exercise, or a small dataset and a question to answer.
  • A case or take-home task. You analyse data and present what you found and what you would recommend.
  • An interview. You may be asked, "Walk me through a project." Interviewers listen for how you chose your method, how you checked your results and how you handled things that went wrong. Increasingly they also ask how you use AI tools and how you verify what they produce.

Proof of work that gets noticed

A good portfolio project is small and complete. It states the question, describes the data, explains what you did, shows the finding, makes a recommendation and says what the analysis cannot tell you. Two or three finished projects are worth more than ten half-finished ones.

To see what this looks like, read the worked examples on our projects page, then practise on the same kind of data using our free datasets and exercises. Make sure that any data you use in a portfolio is public, sample or your own, and never data from an employer or client.

A 90-day plan

Everyone learns at a different pace, so treat this as a starting point rather than a promise.

  1. Days 1 to 30: foundations. Work through Excel and SQL exercises until you can clean data, summarise it and answer simple questions without help.
  2. Days 31 to 60: dashboards and a first project. Learn a dashboard tool and build your first complete project, from question to recommendation.
  3. Days 61 to 90: a second project and job preparation. Build a second project with an AI assistant helping, and record how you checked its output. Write a short summary for each project, update your CV and start applying. Practise explaining each project out loud.

Common mistakes

  • Jumping between tools. Go deep on a few before adding more.
  • Only watching tutorials. You learn when you build something yourself and get stuck.
  • Ignoring the business question. A technically neat analysis that answers the wrong question does not impress.
  • Neglecting communication. Practise writing a one-page summary of every project.
  • Overstating your skills. Be honest about your level. Employers can tell quickly, and honesty about what you are still learning is respected.
